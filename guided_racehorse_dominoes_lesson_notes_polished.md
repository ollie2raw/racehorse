# Guided Racehorse Dominoes Lesson Notes — Polished Draft

## Core Lesson Themes

This fixed guided game is meant to teach players how to think through Racehorse Dominoes the way a stronger player actually sees the board.

The main ideas are:

- **More tiles = more power.** Drawing is not always bad. Extra tiles can create future chains, especially when you are early in the hand.
- **Think in chains, not single moves.** The best move is often the one that starts a sequence, not just the move that scores immediately.
- **Doubles extend control.** A double can keep your turn alive, open branches, and help you turn one good move into several.
- **Avoid crossing opponent doubles when possible.** If your opponent opens a double, crossing it often gives them more lanes and more chances to score.
- **Control the count when no score exists.** When you cannot score, the goal is usually to lower or shape the count so your opponent has fewer clean scoring options.
- **Track endgame outs.** Late in the hand, you need to think about who can go out, what tiles are still live, and whether the boneyard is locked.

# Opening: Drawing for Power and Early Board Control

## Turn 0

There are a few good openings here.

The obvious scoring line is to start with the **6-6**, then play **6-3**, which gives us 3 points right away. That is a totally reasonable move.

But I also notice that we have the **1-1**, and no other 1s in our hand. That matters because if we open with the double 1, we are forced to draw.

In normal dominoes, drawing can feel like a punishment. In Racehorse Dominoes, especially early in the hand, drawing can actually be a weapon. More tiles means more future options, more chances to chain, and more ways to keep control later.

So instead of taking the small early score, I would start with the **1-1** and draw.

**Play: 1-1.**

## Turn 1

We only drew two tiles, but that is still fine. The point of that opening was not to guarantee a huge draw. It was to give ourselves a chance to increase our options early.

Now we play the **1-6** and keep looking for future chain opportunities.

**Play: 1-6.**

## Turn 6

We have a couple of scoring choices here.

We could play **4-5** on the 4 and score 2 points. That works.

But we can also play **5-6** on the 5 and score the same 2 points. The difference is that **5-6** works better with the rest of our hand because it sets up the **6-6**, and then we can follow with **0-4** afterward.

This is the first small example of thinking beyond the current move. If two moves score the same, choose the one that gives your next tile a purpose.

**Play: 5-6 on the 5.**

## Turn 7

Now the setup from the previous move pays off.

We play the **6-6** because doubles keep our turn alive. This also keeps the board working for our hand because we already know we can follow with **0-4** afterward.

**Play: 6-6.**

## Turn 8

Now we finish the little chain by playing **0-4** on the right blank.

This scores 4 points and shows why the earlier **5-6** was better than just taking the same 2 points with **4-5**.

The lesson is simple: when two moves score equally, pick the one that creates the better next move.

**Play: 0-4 on the right blank.**

## Turn 9

At this point, there is no clean scoring move.

When I cannot score, I usually think about lowering or controlling the count so I do not hand my opponent an easy scoring position. Here, I am also worried about my opponent using the **4-4** if I leave the board in the wrong shape.

I would rather play off a 4 because there are fewer 4s available than 6s. That makes it a little harder for Fritz to immediately punish us.

**Play off the 4.**

## Turn 11

Again, there is no scoring move available, so this becomes a positional move.

I want to keep playing off the 4 because it feels safer than opening the 3. If I play **6-3** off the double 6, I risk giving Fritz a strong reply if he has the **3-3**.

So the goal here is not to score. The goal is to avoid opening a number that could let Fritz take over the hand.

**Play off the 4.**

## Turn 13

There is only one move available here, so no real decision to make.

**Play the only legal move.**

## Turn 16

There is no scoring move, but we can go out and end the hand by playing off either the 3 or the 2.

When you can go out, the decision changes. You are no longer just thinking about the count. You are thinking about ending the hand and collecting whatever points are left in your opponent’s tiles.

**Go out.**

# Hand 2: Avoid Crossing Opponent Doubles and Build Chains

## Turn 19

Usually, when your opponent plays a double, you do **not** want to cross it unless you have to.

Here, Fritz has played the double, and the safer idea would usually be to play on the left side of the 5 instead of opening the vertical branches. Crossing a double gives the board more lanes, and more lanes usually means more chances for your opponent to score.

But in this position, we do not have a 4, so we cannot fully avoid breaking the double. Since we have to play off the 5, we should at least take the points.

**Play: 5-6 for 2 points.**

## Turn 20

This is a tough spot.

Our main choices are **3-5** and **2-6**.

The **3-5** opens more numbers and makes the count higher. That can create more action, but it can also give Fritz more scoring chances.

The **2-6** has a downside too: it may let Fritz score with the **4-4**. But even with that risk, I prefer it because it does not open the board as much as **3-5**.

Sometimes the best move is not perfectly safe. You are choosing the move that gives away the least.

**Play: 2-6.**

## Turn 23

Now we have options.

We can score by adding 3 to the count. There are two ways to do that: playing the **3-3** on the single 3, or playing **5-3**.

I prefer **5-3** off the vertical double 5 branch because we also hold the **0-3**. That means this move is not just a score; it helps set up the next part of the chain.

This is exactly how I want you to start looking at the board. Do not only ask, “Which move scores?” Ask, “Which scoring move gives me the next move too?”

**Play: 5-3 off the vertical 5 branch.**

## Turn 24

Now we play the **3-3**.

The reason this works so well is that we already planned for the **0-3**. The double lets us continue the turn, and then we can use the other 3 to keep the count at 10 and score again.

This is the power of chaining: one move scores, the double extends the turn, and the next tile converts the position into more points.

**Play: 3-3.**

## Turn 25

Now we play the **3-0** on the right tail.

This was the follow-up we were building toward. The important part is that this did not happen by accident. We chose the earlier **5-3** because we already saw that this tile could come next.

**Play: 3-0 on the right tail.**

## Turn 26

Only one move is available here.

**Play the only legal move.**

## Turn 28

Only one move is available here, and it lets us go out.

Going out is valuable because you score from your opponent’s remaining tiles. The total pips in their hand are divided by 5, just like normal scoring.

For example, if Fritz had **5-5** and **5-0** left, that would be 15 total pips, so we would score 3 points.

**Play off the 2 and go out.**

I usually like this kind of opening because it gives us more tiles and more future power. There are some hands where I would rather take an immediate score, but I would only do that if I already see a strong scoring chain or if the situation clearly calls for quick points.

In general, especially early: more tiles = more power.

**Play: 3-3 and draw.**

## Turn 30

Only one move is available here.

The **3-5** can go on either side.

**Play: 3-5.**

## Turn 32

## Turn 33

We have a few 6s, so playing **5-6** makes sense. It keeps us connected to a number we can use later.

## Turn 51

So my preference is to play **0-4** on the 4.

**Play: 0-4 on the 4.**

## Turn 53

Here we can really only play **2-6**.

I would not rush to play the **0-0** yet. The double blank is often a useful tile to hold because it can extend a future turn or help build a scoring chain later.

**Play: 1-1.**

# Hand 5: Patience, Long Chains, and a Major Scoring Sequence

## Turn 70

Usually, opening with **0-0** is not ideal because the double blank can be a great tile to save for a later chain.

We could start with **1-4** or **2-3**, but this is another spot where I still like playing the double to draw. Early in the hand, I am willing to give up immediate simplicity to gain more future options.

More tiles = more power.

**Play: 0-0 and draw.**

## Turn 71

Only one move is available here.

**Play: 0-2 on either side.**

## Turn 73

We have a choice here.

Since we have more 3s than 4s, I would rather play **1-3**. This keeps us connected to a number that appears more in our hand.

That is a simple but important rule: when nothing scores, play toward the numbers you can continue using.

**Play: 1-3.**

## Turn 75

When you have a lot of tiles, you do not need to force the action immediately.

This is where patience matters. With a big hand, your goal is often to play positional, block useful lanes, and wait until the board lines up for a larger chain.

Nothing huge stands out here, but I like **1-4** on the right side. Part of the idea is that it may bait Fritz into playing the **3-3**, which would open more numbers and lanes that are useful for our hand.

We are not just trying to score right now. We are trying to shape the board so our bigger hand becomes powerful later.

**Play: 1-4 on the right side.**

## Turn 78

This is the first major position where I want you to start thinking in chains, not just single moves.

Earlier, I talked about how drawing can give you more power because more tiles means more ways to keep your turn alive. This is a great example of that idea.

When I look at this hand, I am not just asking, “What scores right now?” I am asking, “Which move keeps the board working for me so I can keep playing again and again?”

There are a lot of options here, and that can feel overwhelming at first. That is normal. Even strong players can miss long sequences in positions like this. The important thing is not to see every move all at once. The important thing is to recognize the start of a strong chain.

So for this first move, I would begin with **3-5**. It scores 2 points, but more importantly, it starts a sequence where I can keep control of the board and continue chaining moves together.

I already see a longer line from here, but I do not want you to memorize it yet. I will walk through the rest one move at a time so you can understand how the chain develops instead of just copying it.

**Play: 3-5 on either 3.**

## Turn 79

Now we continue the chain.

After **3-5**, I play **2-2**. Doubles are powerful because they let you keep your turn going, so whenever you can use one without losing control, it is usually a strong play.

More importantly, **2-2** keeps the board in a shape that still works for my hand. I am not just playing it because it is available. I am playing it because it leads directly into my next move.

From here, I can follow up with **3-6**, which continues the chain and keeps the board open in a way that favors the tiles I still have.

**Play: 2-2.**

## Turn 80

Now we play **3-6**.

This move was already set up by the **2-2**. When I played the double, I was not just extending my turn. I was making sure I had a clean follow-up.

At this point, the key idea is that each move should already lead into the next one. I am not searching randomly anymore. I am following through on a plan.

**Play: 3-6.**

## Turn 81

Now we continue with **6-6**.

I am playing this because I already know my next move is **2-3**. The **6-6** changes the board total, and then **2-3** adjusts it to land on a scoring number.

Even though these are two separate moves, I am thinking about them together. The first move sets up the total, and the next move converts it into points.

This is an important idea: start paying attention to the value of your tiles and how they change the count. That helps you see which numbers you need to add or remove to score.

**Play: 6-6.**

## Turn 82

Now we play **2-3**.

The previous move set up this scoring move. The **2-3** adjusts the total to land on a scoring number, giving us 5 points.

It also opens the double 2, which lets us continue the chain with another scoring move right after.

**Play: 2-3.**

## Turn 83

Now we finish the scoring part of this sequence with **2-5**.

This is the last clean scoring move in the chain. The important lesson is that the points came from planning several tiles ahead, not from finding one lucky move.

**Play: 2-5.**

## Turn 84

Now we finish with **5-6**, played off the double 6.

We choose this instead of playing it off a 5 because it lowers the count and ends the turn cleanly.

That matters because once the scoring chain is over, we do not want to leave Fritz an easy reply.

**Play: 5-6 off the double 6.**

## Turn 87

We can score here by playing **0-4** on the 0 branch.

This gives us points immediately, and it also sets up our next move with the **4-4**.

Again, the move is valuable because it does two things: it scores now and creates a follow-up.

**Play: 0-4.**

## Turn 88

Now we play our last tile, the **4-4**.

Scoring plays and doubles keep your turn alive. If you empty your hand with one while the boneyard can still be drawn, you must draw and continue. If the boneyard is locked, the hand can end.

That may feel strange at first, but it is part of the game. Sometimes the correct move still forces a draw because drawable tiles remain.

**Play: 4-4.**

## Turn 89

We can score here by adding 1 to the count.

The best move is **4-3** on the 3.

**Play: 4-3 on the 3.**

## Turn 90

Now we lower the count by playing **4-6** off the double 4.

This is not just about getting rid of a tile. It is about ending the turn in a cleaner, safer board shape.

**Play: 4-6 off the double 4.**

# Endgame: Locked Boneyard, Clean Outs, and Final Win

## Turn 95

The boneyard is locked at 2 tiles, and Fritz cannot play, so he has to pass.

That is a huge endgame detail. When the boneyard is locked, your opponent cannot just draw into a solution. If they cannot play, they are stuck.

The board is in a scoring position, and we only have one move left, which means we can go out here.

**Play the final tile and go out.**

## Turn 98

Only one move is available here.

**Play the only legal move.**

## Turn 100

Take the point here by playing **6-0**.

At this stage of the game, small points matter because we are close to the finish.

**Play: 6-0.**

## Turn 101

Only one move is available here.

**Play the only legal move.**

## Turn 104

I would go with **4-1** here.

The **1-5** is already out, but the **6-5** and **2-5** are not. That means playing the other option may leave Fritz better access to live 5s.

The **4-1** keeps better control of the remaining tiles and avoids giving away the cleaner scoring route.

**Play: 4-1.**

This is it. We can win the game here.

We can go out cleanly by chaining our last three tiles in this order: **6-4**, then **1-1**, and finally **4-2**.

The important thing is to see that the game is no longer about maximizing a huge chain. It is about finding the clean winning route.

Start with **6-4**.

**Play: 6-4.**

## Turn 107

That move scored the 2 points we needed to reach the final score of 60.

Now we continue the turn with **1-1**, and then finish the game by going out with **4-2**.

The double keeps the turn alive, which lets us complete the final sequence.

**Play: 1-1.**

## Turn 108

Finish the game by going out with **4-2**.

This is the clean ending: score the final points, use the double to continue, and go out with the last tile.

**Play: 4-2 and win.**

# Final Teaching Summary

This guided game is built around one main habit: stop looking for only the current best tile and start seeing how tiles connect.

The strongest moves usually do one of three things:

1. **Score while setting up another move.**
2. **Use a double to keep control.**
3. **Shape the board so the opponent has fewer clean replies.**

Early in the game, drawing can be powerful because more tiles create more future chains. In the middle of the game, the goal is to recognize when a small scoring move can become a longer sequence. Late in the game, the focus shifts to outs, locked boneyards, and clean ways to finish.

That is the mindset I want players to learn from this lesson: **more tiles create power, doubles create control, and chains win games.**
